Retraction Report

Authored by William Phinney · 21 October 2026

This report, prepared for Retraction Watch, primarily examines 48,644 journal paper retractions. In this dataset, roughly two in five retractions are the result of misconduct. Business and technology papers are the most likely to be retracted for misconduct (58.5%), while basic life sciences, health sciences and physical sciences are the least likely (37.2%, 38.7% and 40.2%; counting each field a paper lists, as in Table 2).

How long a retraction takes depends mostly on how the case is handled: for example, journal investigations and image checks add 78-89%, while the notice being on the original DOI cuts it by 62%.

Once you look within fair cohorts, time-to-retraction has slowly increased with time, mostly driven by an increased prevalence of journal investigation, and other lengthy processes, as the reason for retraction. Equalizing reason, there is no steady trend in time-to-retraction.

We recommend a series of changes and initiatives, mostly aimed at increasing the quantity and reliability of information gathered about each paper (see Proposed plan).

Prior to working with the dataset, we conducted a preliminary review of the literature on this topic. However, research on appropriate topics is light, and many of the papers on the topic use flawed methods. The papers listed in Literature & expectations are among the better found.

1Introduction

Only retracted papers are in the dataset, so while we can describe who publishes papers which are retracted, we are unable to judge, for example, the percentage of papers retracted by country. Thus, a country having a high count of retractions is not necessarily an indicator that it is particularly inclined towards fraud.

Fig. 1, the retraction clock, view 1 of 7. Retractions were rare through the 2000s. Each paper rises from its publication date until it is retracted; grey: not yet retracted.

This dataset includes 14,070 conference paper retractions. Roughly three in five (61%) of these retractions fall between 2010 and 2011, and only 8.7% of conference papers are retracted due to misconduct.

Conference paper retractions do not behave like the rest of the dataset, and are not important to our work. By default, they will not be shown.

Fig. 1, view 2 of 7. IEEE's conference retractions arrive in a handful of huge batches.

Retractions have grown with time; only 1,124 papers were retracted in 2012, roughly a fifth of 2022's 5,584.

Many papers are retracted in mass batches of 100 or more retractions; here, seventy such batches contain 23,692 papers, 36% of the dataset.

Fig. 1, view 3 of 7. From 2012, retractions climb year after year.

Retractions peaked at 13,193 in 2023, the result of Hindawi's 9,673 2023 retractions.

Fig. 1, view 4 of 7. 2023 is the record year, driven by Hindawi's mass retractions.

No paper can be retracted before it is published, so any recently-published paper in the dataset must also be retracted quickly. You can adjust for this, and we do, but unfortunately and irreducibly we know less about recent periods of time.

Fig. 1, view 5 of 7. Recent papers can only appear here if they were retracted quickly. Dashed curve: the ceiling, the time left before 11 Sep 2026.

The database contains some gaps; 6,759 papers contain 'date unknown' codes (4,105 of these are conference papers); 1,761 papers are listed as retracted zero days after being published (the majority of these carry the date unknown code, and/or were retracted on the original DOI; but a further 541 remain once conference and unknown-date papers are set aside); 18.1% of all papers give limited or no information about the reason, meaning misconduct is likely undercounted; 5,710 papers are missing their original DOI; 2,138 papers were published on January first (you would expect ~180 such papers), meaning some papers likely have placeholder dates.

Fig. 1, view 6 of 7. Three groups behave unlike the rest: conference papers, unknown dates and same-day retractions.

Removing the categories we exclude by default (conference, unknown date, same-day retraction) reduces the paper count to 48,644, increases the misconduct share from 34.7% to 42.8%, and increases the median wait from 488 to 644 days.

Fig. 1, view 7 of 7. Setting those three groups aside leaves the papers used from here on. Set-aside papers wait on the rail below; click a label, a chip or a Table 1 row to bring them back.

2Summary statistics

We consider any paper tagged "is_misconduct" to be retracted due to misconduct; this tag is added if the retraction includes any of these thirteen reason codes: Paper Mill; Plagiarism of/in Article; Plagiarism of Text; Plagiarism of Image; Plagiarism of Data; Euphemisms for Plagiarism; Falsification/Fabrication of Data; Falsification/Fabrication of Image; Falsification/Fabrication of Results; False/Forged Authorship; False/Forged Affiliation; Rogue Editor; Taken via Peer Review.

We are left with 20,834 misconduct retractions and 27,810 non-misconduct retractions. The median time to retraction is almost identical, respectively 658 and 631 days. Misconduct retractions have slightly less variance in their time-to-retraction (middle half 438–1,161 and 317–1,455 days), but the shape is similar for both.

Fig. 2, misconduct vs not, view 1 of 5. Misconduct and other retractions take a similar median time; other retractions are more spread out. Black rules: medians.

There is significant variation in percentage of papers retracted for misconduct by field (counting each paper once in each field it lists):

Business and technology retractions are 58.5% misconduct, similar to Humanities 56.4% and Social sciences 51.2%. On the other hand, Basic life sciences retractions are 37.2% misconduct, and Health sciences 38.7%.

44.6% of misconduct papers list a B/T subject vs 23.7% of non-misconduct papers.

Fig. 2, view 2 of 5. Business and technology retractions are the most often misconduct. One column per paper (its most common subject); width: papers; orange: misconduct share.

The vast majority of the retractions in the dataset are research articles; however, non-research article retractions (mostly reviews, clinical studies and meta-analyses) are slightly less likely to be the result of misconduct.

Fig. 2, view 3 of 5. Research articles dominate both groups. One column per paper.

Retractions associated with China-based authors are 63.1% of misconduct retractions but only 44.1% of non-misconduct retractions.

Retractions with Chinese authors are 51.7% retracted for misconduct, vs. 50.4% for India, 26.5% for the US, and 27.6% for Iran. Retractions with authors from multiple countries are less likely to be the result of misconduct (35.6% vs 44.6% single-country).

Fig. 2, view 4 of 5. Papers from China are the largest group, and more than half carry a misconduct code. Multi-country: two or more countries listed.

Hindawi alone accounts for 24% of the papers shown, and 65.6% of these retractions are the result of misconduct; this is remarkably high, and yet still not the highest rate among major publishers (Spandidos, with 917 papers, retracts 73.3% for misconduct). IOS Press retractions were 60.6% the result of misconduct; Springer 48.5%; Elsevier 33.1%; Wiley 28.1%. 42.4% of misconduct retractions came in mass batches, but only 15.2% of the non-misconduct retractions did.

The most commonly listed reasons for retraction were Investigation by Journal/Publisher (on 53.1% of papers), Unreliable Results (43.6%) and Investigation by Third Party (34.0%); Paper Mill appears on 22.9%.

Duplication of/in Image is the slowest common reason (median 1,687 days).

Fig. 2, view 5 of 5. Hindawi is both the largest publisher here and the most misconduct-heavy. The seven largest publishers, plus the rest.

3Regression analysis

3.1Regression form

On a raw scale, the histogram of days-to-retraction is highly skewed (2.41). The median is 644, the mean is 1,050, and the max is 9,379.

Fig. 3, days to retraction, view 1 of 2. Waits are heavily right-skewed.

Taking the log of days helps with skew (now -.58, but in practice better than this; the remaining skew comes from a tail of very fast retractions, not truncation).

(We use log(days + 1) rather than log(days) to allow visualization of same-day retractions on the same scale, should the reader wish.)

Fig. 3, view 2 of 2. On a log scale the waits are close to symmetric, so the model uses log days. From here on, days use this log axis.

A paper's age is taken from publication to 11 Sep 2026, the last retraction on file. Age has a skew of 1.20, while log age's skew is .07; the model uses log age as a result. Age encodes both differences in retraction speed over time and the mechanically increased possible time to retract a paper (mostly the latter; see 3.3).

Fig. 4, age vs days, view 1 of 3. No paper can wait longer than it has existed. Dashed: the ceiling (days = age).

Log age beats raw or quadratic age (oos R² 0.342 vs 0.319 raw, 0.333 quadratic), and while raw and log authors are functionally identical (both round to .342), log authors is used, as an elasticity is preferable.

Beyond the three baseline predictors (paper age, author count and misconduct; R² 0.160), we consider whether or not the retraction was published on the original DOI, if the retraction resulted from a) journal investigation, b) image duplication, or c) unreliable results, if the retraction came with a limited notice, and if the publisher was Hindawi.

This nine-term model produces an R² of .342, an s_e of .851 (s_y of 1.049). Out of sample R² is .342, vs. .340 for an unseen batch, .337 for an unseen journal, and .296 for an unseen retraction year. This indicates negligible overfitting. Every VIF is under two.

Fig. 4, view 2 of 3. Older papers waited longer; misconduct papers sit slightly higher. Lines: the model's predictions, other terms at their means.

3.2Regression diagnostics

Quantitative Variables: met, as predictors are log age, log authors, and seven indicator variables, while the response variable is log(days + 1); the response and both continuous predictors are quantitative, and the yes/no indicators enter the model directly.

Linearity: met; the average residual stays within .12 log-units across the fitted range, and within .14 and .07 log units across log age and log authors respectively.

Equal Variance: fails, and it isn't particularly close; the youngest quintile of papers has less than half the spread of the oldest quintile (SD .56 vs. SD 1.19). Hindawi's sweeps are notable here (SD .31 vs. SD .96). However, we report bootstrapped standard errors, which do not assume one common spread.

Fig. 5, diagnostics, view 1 of 4. Residuals average close to zero across the fitted range. Line: average residual.

Nearly Normal: fails, as the residuals are left-skewed (-1.25) and have a more dominant peak than a normal curve (75% within ±1 sₑ, vs. 68%), with a heavy lower tail (1.42% below −3 sₑ, vs. 0.13%). This results from a segment of papers pulled much more quickly than the model expects. Given our n, the coefficients' sampling distributions remain exceedingly normal, and the bootstrap does not assume normal errors in any case; however, this invalidates interval predictions for individual papers.

Fig. 5, view 2 of 4. The residuals have a heavier lower tail than a normal curve. Dashed line: where normal residuals would fall.

Independence: fails. These papers are not a random sample, but close to every retraction ever issued that meets our criteria. This is not exactly the situation these methods are aimed at, but we persevere: to account for high in-publisher-batch correlation (.63) we resample by batch (increasing SEs by 50-190%). Papers in the same journal are also correlated (.35); as a check, clustering on journal instead roughly doubles the SEs again, and every term remains significant (highest p = .001).

Fig. 5, view 3 of 4. Residuals within a batch move together, so papers retracted together are not independent. Ticks: batch averages; dashed funnel: where they would fall if papers were independent.

Outliers, leverage, and influence: no worrying results here. No paper is particularly important (largest Cook's d = .004). Refitting without the 691 most extreme results produces no meaningful change.

Collinearity: nothing is worryingly collinear (no VIF at two or above).

P-values: given our n, raw p values are near-zero. With bootstrapped standard errors, p-values rise by many orders of magnitude but remain near zero (largest p = 5 × 10⁻¹⁰, misconduct). That is, if misconduct had no association with time-to-retraction once the other terms are held fixed, an estimate this far from zero would essentially never occur by chance. This speaks to certainty, not size; size is covered below.

Fig. 5, view 4 of 4. No single paper has much influence on the model. Dashed lines: 4/n (Cook's distance) and 2p/n (leverage).

3.3Interpret coefficients

A ten percent older paper is associated with a 9.4% higher time-to-retraction. From the first to the third quartile of age (4.3 to 11.3 years), the predicted wait rises from 418 to 1,034 days (+147%). This is large, but the cause isn't clear: if the real behavior was that retraction time was a constant percentage of the ceiling maximum retraction date, this would be 10% (vs. 9.4% as measured), and thus this could either be a real and large time-effect, or a more simple behavior along with a very small time effect.

In fact, the latter is true; running a regression without a differing ceiling (see below) drops age to 1.3% per 10% older.

Fig. 6, what-if from Q1 to Q3, or No to Yes. Every paper's predicted wait, as one predictor moves from its first to its third quartile (yes/no predictors: from No to Yes). Drag or toggle to try it yourself.

The 1.3% that remains holds the type of case fixed. Much of the raw rise in waits is actually a change in what leads to retraction: journal investigations went from 9% of retractions of 2010 papers to 75% of retractions of 2021 papers. Remove Hindawi's papers, and even the 1.3% shrinks to 0.5% per 10% older, indistinguishable from zero (p = 0.19).

Fig. 4, view 3 of 3: the age check. Give every paper the same three years to be retracted, and most of the age effect disappears.

A doubling of team size (author count) is associated with 5.1% longer time-to-retraction. From 2 to 6 authors (Q1 to Q3), the predicted wait rises from 623 to 675 days (+8%). This is a real, but small, effect. This is likely due to increased difficulty and delay in investigation, correspondence, and so forth. (This vindicates H5.)

The retraction being the result of misconduct is associated with a 14% longer time-to-retraction. This is small, but meaningful. This is higher in the three-term-model (24%), as some process codes encode information about this variable. On all papers in the dataset this increases significantly, driven by conference and unknown-date papers, but the full dataset is not very amenable to regression modeling. Such a difference is notable, but does not call these results into question.

The retraction occurring on the original DOI is associated with a 62% shorter time-to-retraction. If something is retracted on the original DOI, it is more often than not a very fast withdrawal.

The retraction being the result of a journal investigation is associated with a 78% longer time-to-retraction. Investigations take time, and journals move slowly.

The retraction being the result of image duplication is associated with an 89% higher time-to-retraction. This is the slowest common process in the dataset.

The retraction being the result of unreliable results is associated with a 53% longer time-to-retraction. Such issues take time to spot and investigate.

The retraction having a limited notice is associated with a 34% lower time-to-retraction. Opaque notices tend to be quick.

The retracted paper being published by Hindawi is associated with a 37% shorter time-to-retraction. The mass cleaning that occurred here caught many papers well before a 'typical' retraction is caught, and the scale brought a journal-side urgency to the process.

4Trends

Raw median wait by publication year has fallen steadily with time, from 3,926 days for papers published in 2000 to 223 days for papers published in 2025. However, this is primarily driven by the ceiling (papers from late 2025 had at most about 260 days in which to be retracted). Steen et al. note this, but don't treat it as serious enough to undermine their comparison; we disagree, and do not think you should take this figure to mean anything, beyond exactly what it says taken quite narrowly.

Fig. 7, trends, view 1 of 5. Raw medians fall over time only because recent papers have not had time to wait long. Line: median per publication year.

The fair comparison shows time-to-retraction inching up over time (322 days-to-retraction for 2010 papers, 459 days-to-retraction for 2021 papers). In sixteen out of seventeen years, misconduct cases took longer to be retracted.

Fig. 7, view 2 of 5. With equal follow-up, misconduct cases take longer than other retractions in almost every year. Individually handled retractions within 3 years of publication, cohorts to 2021.

But even this comparison does not report what it seems to: once you hold the type of case fixed, the increase functionally disappears, going just about dead even (with the exception of 2022's unusually low result, the result of Hindawi's mass cleaning). The rise in the fair comparison was driven by increased prevalence of journal investigations as retraction-cause; these journal investigations take notably longer to retract than other causes for retraction typically do.

Fig. 7, view 3 of 5. Comparing like with like, typical waits show no steady trend since 2005. Hover a year for its case mix.

Fig. 7, view 4 of 5: Lexis diagram. Retractions arrive in campaigns: each horizontal line is one publisher clearing many papers on one day. Across: publication date; up: retraction date; ink: mass retractions.

The quantity of retractions has increased over time, going from 1,124 in 2012 to 5,584 in 2022 to 13,193 in 2023 (all retractions, including the groups set aside). However, if you exclude mass retractions, 2023 has 3,385, fewer than 2022 (4,311) or 2024 (4,418). While the pure quantity has clearly increased since 2010, the continuous flow seems to be roughly flat for the last few years, with variation mostly caused by inconsistent although relatively frequent mass retractions.

Fig. 7, view 5 of 5. Mass retractions come in a few waves from a few publishers. Mass retractions: 100+ papers, one publisher, one day.

5Beyond the linear model

It's difficult for a regression model to explain time-to-retraction in this dataset.

Fig. 8, beyond the linear model, view 1 of 3. The actual waits.

Even a nine-term linear model is only able to predict values in a number of narrow bands, and it doesn't capture interactions between factors.

Fig. 8, view 2 of 3. The linear model squeezes its predictions into a narrow band. Each dot moves to the linear model's prediction.

A more flexible model, a blend of four ML approaches, explains 64.6% of the variation in the dataset. It learns to understand this dataset during this period in history, and even when tested on an unseen journal or unseen batch of papers, it significantly outperforms a linear model (R² .518, .588 vs. .337, .340 respectively).

However, as the lead comes primarily from this better understanding, it drops back to the performance of the regression model when testing on unseen years (blended R² .273, 9-term R² .296).

The 64.6% can be said to be best understood, then, as a limit on what can be predicted in this dataset during this time period. It also shows just how much retraction timing depends on publisher campaigns, which is the most significant and durable finding in this report.

Fig. 8, view 3 of 3. The super model tracks individual waits far more closely, but its edge disappears for a year it has never seen. Each dot moves to the super model's out-of-sample prediction.

6Limitations and data needed

  • We don't know anything about non-retracted papers, which means we can't produce many useful values; for example, looking at retraction rates by country, or retraction rates over time, would be extremely interesting.
  • As 18.1% of the notices in this dataset give limited or no information about the reason, misconduct is almost necessarily undercounted.
  • 6,759 papers have unknown dates (2,654, once conference papers are set aside), and we exclude them all; if these behave differently than the rest of this data (as they almost definitionally must), being forced to exclude these, we gain a less complete understanding of the dataset.
  • Mass retraction batches so heavily drive the trends, and contain so much of the data, and yet are irreducibly individual events not really suited to regression analysis. As the number of these batches is large enough, the regression results should still hold, but this certainly reduces how confident we can be in our results, and in their continuance into the future.
  • We lack any way to distinguish detection time from processing time (PubPeer posts, expressions of concern, etc.).
  • Misconduct, defined as it is here, does not allow us to look at error vs. fraud, and we do not have data which would allow us to even attempt to determine this.

7Proposed plan for Retraction Watch

We recommend that Retraction Watch:

  • Push for increased date precision, especially looking to reduce placeholder dates and unknown dates.
  • Tag mass-retractions with an id, allowing for these events to be understood and analyzed with more precision.
  • Look to increase the variety of possible reason codes, and push the industry to give more detailed reasons (or, to give reasons at all, as almost one in five notices currently give limited or no information).
  • Create author IDs, or push for their creation, to track similarly-named authors more carefully, particularly to allow for better tracking and understanding of serial offenders.
  • Attempt to gather data separating time-to-detection and time-to-retraction.

8Conclusion

In summary, we can say the following about retractions in this dataset:

  • Two in five retractions are the result of misconduct.
  • China- and India-based authors' retractions are more likely to be the result of misconduct in comparison to other high-raw-retraction-count countries' authors.
  • Retractions for misconduct take longer than retractions for other reasons, but not by very much.
  • Adjusting for the ceiling effect, it has taken longer for papers to be retracted as time goes on. However, this effect is mostly driven by newer papers being retracted for reasons which take longer (by their nature), and once you account for this there has been little change in the pace of retraction over time.
  • A large minority of retractions occur in batches, and as a result it is difficult to disentangle commonalities in the idiosyncratic features of these batches and real changes to how retractions occur over time.
